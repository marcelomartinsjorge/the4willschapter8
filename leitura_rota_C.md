PART I

The Full Plate
✦

Redom buries its own where they fall.

SOLDIERS’ SAYING

### [1]
The plate goes back full.

Bread, a cold chicken leg, three plums. Tobias takes the tray without hurrying. He has served my table for twenty-six years, and for eighty-one days he has carried the tray back the way he brought it.

He speaks to the bread:

— The kitchen sends word there’s lamb tomorrow, Highness.

— Tomorrow I eat on the road.

He bows his head and goes. The door closes with its usual care.

The chair in my room faces the window. I have never moved it: it is the only corner where I can sit at ease.

Since the news of Simon’s death, I haven’t felt hungry. Thirsty, yes. The Judge forgive me, but it is a thirst for revenge.

I sit in the chair and look down at the White Capital. Beautiful, orderly, clean. I put my fingers to my neck and count my pulse. Fifty-two.

### [4]
My mother no longer leaves her bed. The curtains stay shut day and night, and the maid keeps a candle lit beside her, in the daytime too.

She hears me come in and doesn’t open her eyes.

— Sit, Laura.

I sit on the edge of the bed. She finds my hand without looking for it.

— Your father rode out of that gate fourteen times. He came back thirteen. Your brother rode out once. You’ve ridden out twenty times and come back twenty…

Her thumb moves over my calluses and stops on the middle one.

— This is the twenty-first time.

My pulse climbs in my neck. My hands move inside hers, and she feels it, and says nothing.

— I still remember you as a girl. Fragile. — She almost smiles. — Maybe you were never fragile. I pictured you as Mother-Queen, like me. You became far more than I knew how to imagine.

She coughs, and the cough hurts her. She goes on anyway:

— If you don’t come back, I’ll curse that Tournament until the last of the few days I have left. You know that, don’t you?

### [10]
The south gate opens with the sun. I count by company, as I have for twenty years: twenty companies of a hundred. Two thousand.

The cavalry in front. Then the Third, Lord Varr’s heavy foot, with Varr walking in the middle of it, because a Varr marches with his own. Joseph rides with his left hand on the reins. His right shakes in the mornings; it already shook at the Tournament.

Ansel rides in the last row of the cavalry. He was a sentry at the Gullets, and he saw my brother fall. He asked to stay in the Capital. I ordered him to come. He is going to show me where it happened.

Behind the column come the wagons: the barrels, the waterskins, the kitchen, the forge on wheels, the stable boys leading the spare horses, the healer’s helpers sitting on the canvas. Those don’t go into my count. Servants aren’t counted.

I mount the grey. Before I pull the rein, he turns his head toward the south road.

PART II

The Road
✦

Before the desert, an army counts its swords. In the desert, it counts drops of water.

KASSIAN D’ORROSE, GENERAL-KING

### [12]
On the first night of the road, the kitchen sets up the big pot in the middle of the camp, and the soldiers line up with their bowls.

I get in line. On the road I eat from the soldiers’ pot. It has always been that way, but the food has never tasted like this.

I promised my mother.

The cook fills my bowl to the brim, because it’s mine.

I eat standing, watching the line move. A corporal of the Third sees my empty bowl and nudges the man beside him. That one nudges the next.

I get back in line. At the third bowl, I promise myself it’s the last.

Joseph sits by the fire with a wineskin and turns the skin in his hand before every swallow.

— The last campaign, Highness. — He raises the skin to me. — Forty years ago I opened the Grakh line in the desert. I want to finish there.

His right hand shakes on the leather of the skin. His left holds the neck. His men look at me.

### [16]
PART III

The Sand
✦

In the desert, whoever carries the water carries the army.

QUARTERMASTERS’ SAYING

### [17]
On the first night in the sand, someone gets into our water.

In the morning, the cavalry troughs are dry, and the hay beneath them is wet. Two horses lying down don’t get up again.

The quartermaster checks the stable barrels. A day’s water is missing.

In the desert, water is worth more than gold. This is no accident.

I have everyone who slept near the stables brought to me.

Servants aren’t counted, and I have never known any of their names. Today, I ask.

They are Wil, the stable boy, who sleeps in the hay, and Tasso, a soldier of the Third who was on guard nearby.

I ask, hard:

— Who emptied the troughs?

They look at me, indignant, with no answer.

I am going to understand what happened. I look at each of them, up close.

I send them all back to work.

Tasso lied about the water, and his mouth isn’t thirsty.

I have no proof. I have what I saw.

### [25]
PART IV

The Gullets
✦

The Gullets post has no wall. It has a well and a wheel.

REPORT TO THE COUNCIL OF REDOM

### [26]
The Gullets appear at the end of the eleventh day: the stake fence, the Grakh’s old wall around the deepest well, the wheel above the well and the red banner with the scales, which someone has washed.

Of the hundred and twenty men of the post, thirty-one are left. They are lined up at the fence gate. The oldest barely has a beard.

My soldiers drink from the well until their bellies hurt. I let them.

I drink too. The other thirst stays.

My father died on this ground when I was fifteen, on the day of an eclipse. The men who came back told my mother the last thing he shouted. I was behind the door.

— Steady! It’s only the dark! Hold the line!

My brother died here too. The news took eight days to reach the Capital, and it took me ninety-two to get here. It feels as if it all happened in the same week.

### [27]
At the Gullets, the nights go quiet. The waterskins are hard with water at dawn, the troughs are full, and the kitchen barrels taste of barrel.

Here water is plentiful. Whatever it is, it doesn’t need to take any now.

### [28]
Ansel leads me to the middle of the yard, between the well and the fence. He stops at a place where the sand is like the sand everywhere else.

— Here, my lady. — He points a hand’s width in front of his boot. — He fell here. The big one knelt there. I was on my horse, by the gate.

— The big one kept watching him until he stopped. His hand went to his chest. He tapped once, with two fingers.

— Once?

— Once, my lady. The second never came.

Two fingers on the chest, two taps: one more round. It was our sign in the orange grove, when he was fourteen and didn’t know how to lose. He asked for one more round, and only got halfway through asking.

— The night before, he gave me half his bread. He said tomorrow’s bread was on him.

### [29]
The well wheel is made of wagon timber, with a thick rope that runs down to the water. Beside it, a crank worn smooth in the middle from so many hands.

I take the crank. It was to build this wheel that Simon came to the Gullets.

The men of the post stop talking while I turn it. Or they don’t, and it’s the world around me that goes quiet.

I remember Simon small. The nights in the orange grove. The strong man he became, the way my mother said he would when he started eating like a grown man.

When I let go, my eyes are wet. The last time I nearly cried was before the Royal Tournament, when I still didn’t know my fate.

Crying in front of my men, the best of them, would be betraying the Judge and the Knight. I pull myself together.

### [30]
They buried my brother on top of the Grakh’s old wall, at the highest point of the post, where you can see the wheel. A pile of stones, and his banner rolled on its staff, buried standing.

I kneel beside the stones.

— Simon.

I tap two fingers on my chest. Once, the way he did. Then the second, the one that never came.

I draw his sword from my belt and rest the hilt against the stones. — It’s coming back dirty, brother.

Behind me, Ansel kneels too and sets a piece of bread on the stones.

My pulse beats high in my neck. My hands won’t keep still on my knee. I stay until they do.

When I stand, I see another grave down below, at the foot of the wall. Older.

### [33]
The scouts come back at the end of the afternoon, their horses lathered.

— The Pyre is at the Last Swallow, Blade. Three days from here, if we’re quick. All of them: the fighters, the old ones, the young. Filling skins.

Last Swallow is what our maps call the last well before the black rock. From there on, the sand ends, and so does the water, and the ground climbs, hard and volcanic, to the peaks where Azrahekt, the Hungry Sun, sleeps in the books of legend.

Not even the Grakh live there. They have stopped at the well to fill their skins and tend their wounded. After that, they’ll turn some way or other, and I’ll lose the trail.

I have the horn sounded. We march as fast as we can.

### [34]
PART V

The Last Swallow
✦

The last chance to drink water for anyone going on. And it tastes of rotten eggs.

TEODRIC VANE, EXPLORER OF REDOM

### [35]
From the top of the dune, at the end of the third day, the Last Swallow: a shallow stone valley with seven wells cut into the rock, and only one of them shining.

Around the one that shines, the Pyre’s fires.

I count from afar. If my eyes don’t fail me, there are thirty-four fires. Varr, beside me, counts thirty-seven. It makes no difference.

The ones in front, near the water, have big shadows around them: the fighters. The ones behind, smaller: the old and the young.

Thirty-four, or thirty-seven. Twenty years ago, at the Gullets, I counted more than three hundred.

The other six wells lie between the dune and the water, open in the ground. A soldier drops a stone into the nearest. It falls, and falls, and hits nothing.

### [43]
PART VI

The Eaten Day
✦

Day be eaten, Biggest be born.

SAYING OF THE OLD GRAKH

### [44]
The face slips.

First the skin of the neck, in thin blue-grey plates. Then the ears, which peel away from the head and open into fins. The body shrinks inside the servant’s clothes until the sleeve covers the hand. The two soldiers let go of the arms at the same moment.

I know this face from a whole afternoon in the Deep Ward.

She looks at me, then at the column on the crest, from one end to the other, slowly, the way I looked at it at the south gate.

— You brought the best, Laura.

She opens her hands.

— And the Dry Mouth drinks them all.

### [45]
In the valley, the well that was shining goes dark.

The water comes out of it all at once, standing up, taller than the dune, and comes up the valley toward her hands, passing over the dry wells.

— Shields! And kill her! Before anything else, kill her!

The Third closes the wall.

The wave hits the crest. It tears away the line of servants, the spare horses, the first rank. The second holds on its knees, water to the waist.

When it goes back, the water carries what it tore away down the slope, and the dry wells drink.

I hear the screams going down into the stone, until they’re gone.

### [48]
After the water, dazed, I hear the beat.

Beats. Three, a silence, and three.

To the west, on the crest of the next dune, men of bronze appear. Ten. Twenty. A hundred? More. They walk in the same step, unhurried, and the beat breaks: four beats, no silence.

The doubled watch on the western flank turns its shields toward them before I give the order.

They cross the mud of the valley without sinking. A sergeant drives his spear into the chest of the first. The spear goes in, and the bronze keeps walking.

What the hell is happening?!

### [49]
Down below, the Pyre saw the water hit us and the bronze come down from the west. The Grakh climb the dune. In front of them, one taller than all the rest, with a mace.

Their leader. Not just taller: more muscled too. The kind of disadvantage that has never been a disadvantage for me.

This is for you, Simon. And for you, Father.

An arrow from far off brings down the sergeant beside me. Back there, on top of a rock, a Grakh with a bow is drawing the next.

Beside the big one, an older Grakh with an old cut on his arm clubs everything that moves.

### [50]
The bronze hits the Third’s line like a door slamming. The spear goes in and won’t come out. A shield holds one blow, and the second drives the shield into the arm holding it.

— The joints! Hammers on the joints!

A sergeant swings his war hammer into the knee of one of them. The stone joint bursts into dust, and the bronze goes down on one knee, and keeps striking from its knees until three men tear its arm from the shoulder.

They don’t scream, they don’t fall back, they don’t stop for the fallen. The one behind steps on the fallen one, in the same beat.

From the side of the water, the Pyre’s drums. The Grakh come in through the holes the bronze tears in our line, with maces, and everything of Redom that has its back turned dies.

And in the middle of it all, whenever the noise slackens, I hear the crack of her water.

### [51]
Near the crest, the Nymae conjurer fights with a dagger in one hand and, in the other, a rope of water as long as a wagon whip, that cracks and cuts. Wherever the whip cracks, ten men are gone.

The only reason it isn’t more is that half the column is facing the bronze, with its back to her.

After some ten lashes, the water runs out. She pulls more from the mud, and less comes. The Dry Mouth has drunk hers as well.

Varr reaches her with six men of the Third. The first dies. The second goes into the mud, and the mud closes over him. Varr doesn’t.

His sword goes in under her arm, between the ribs. A soldier raises his axe for her neck, and she puts the dagger in his throat first.

Opened, bleeding, she still slips two blows, like a dancer, and cuts Redom’s men like paper.

Varr wrenches his sword free, raises it over his head and brings it down with all the rage he has, top to bottom. This time, she falls.

Varr raises his sword and shouts his own name to one side of the valley, then to the other.

### [52]
I can’t see the conjurer any more. I see the big one coming my way, and my men getting out of his path, because whoever doesn’t, falls. And because they know I want him.

I get down from the grey and slap his haunch. He runs for the back of the dune. On foot I read better.

He is taller than any Grakh I have killed in twenty years. Grey skin, lower teeth jutting out of his mouth, an animal’s tooth hanging from a cord at his neck. In his eyes I recognise what I feel inside: the will to kill.

He stops ten paces away and looks at me.

It’s Ansel’s big one. I don’t need to ask.

I draw Simon’s sword.

He finally falls to his knees. Then onto his side. The mace slides out of his hand.

Behind his thigh, the cut I opened pours blood into the sand.

He turns his head back toward the Pyre’s camps and keeps it turned that way. I don’t know who he’s looking for.

On my arm, blood runs down to my hand. It’s mine. It has been twenty years since I saw my own. I feel more blood running inside my armour, under the plates the mace dented.

I hate lying, to myself most of all: I was nearly beaten. But I won.

It is a relief I haven’t felt since my fight against Joseph, at the Royal Tournament.

### [53]
I stand over him. He breathes through his open mouth, teeth bared.

He was an excellent opponent. But now the Grakh end. No one else of my family, no one else of Redom, dies at the hands of these savages, in the sand of this desert.

I raise the sword, still looking at him.

The ground darkens, as if a dragon were passing over us, but the shadow doesn’t pass.

I look up. A black piece comes in at the sun’s edge and keeps closing, and the light thins. An eclipse.

Behind me, Redom cries out:

— Eclipse! Eclipse!

### [54]
I turn toward them with my sword held high.

— Steady! It’s only the dark! Hold the line!

The words are my father’s. The line closes.

When I turn back, the fallen one is no longer alone.

### [55]
Standing between me and the fallen Grakh, in the dark of the day turned to night, is a Grakh I have never seen. An abomination.

He is bigger than the other. Much bigger. Bigger than any. One arm is thicker than the other, and the bone of the shoulder is still rising under the skin, grey skin, cracked, with muscle where no Grakh has muscle.

An arrow from the Third goes into his chest. He doesn’t even seem to notice. He looks at me.

He laughs.

The sword is heavy. At sixteen, it was this heavy.

My pulse beats in my neck. It beats in my throat. It beats in my teeth.

The Grakh stop fighting. They look at him, beat their chests and shout a word I know from prisoners: the Biggest.

### [56]
On the crest, there are still many bows.

— Archers! On me!

He comes.

The men keep shooting. At the last volley, he falls to his knees. There is more arrow than skin on him, hundreds of shafts driven into his body, and he is still laughing, and trying to get up. That sight I will never forget.

Then the laughter stops. The body goes down little by little, as if it were still deciding whether to give.

I go close to make sure he isn’t breathing. Inside him, a faint light shines from within, outward, and the flesh comes apart around it, slowly, almost too slowly to see.

That beast was not of our world.

The sun comes back at the edge.

### [57]
With the sun, the valley appears.

The last man of bronze is still walking, on one leg, dragging itself by the arm it has left, in the same beat. Three men of the Third take it apart on the ground, with hammers. After that, the beat stops.

Not one of them fell back. Not one Grakh, either.

Shattered bronze from one end of the valley to the other, and among the bronze, ours and theirs, one on top of the other. Nearly two thousand of ours.

Near the crest, in the mud, her body is still there, its head split open. Inside it shines the same faint light as the Biggest, and the flesh comes apart around it, like ash the wind carries off a little at a time.

I go to where the big one fell. He is lying the same way, turned toward the camps, in the middle of a pool that has stopped spreading. Nobody loses that much blood and gets up.

I brought down their leader. Or I brought down a big one, and the big one was the other.

On the ground, my blood and his are drying together.

### [58]
Those who can get up, get up. I walk among them and put my hand on each one.

Fifty-six. Out of two thousand.

Varr, his head bound in a strip of his own cloak, tells the death of the witch to anyone who will listen. When he finishes, he starts again. Nobody laughs.

### [59]
We go back to the Gullets on foot. The horses that are left carry those who can’t walk. Of the way, I only remember the sun and the sand.

Joseph is at the fence gate, both hands on his belt. He looks at each man who comes in. When the last one has passed, he keeps waiting for one more, who doesn’t come.

Someone puts a bowl in my hand. It’s full.

I hold the bowl. In the Capital, my mother will count twenty-one.
